# Sentence-Level Tension and Voice — Research Notes

Working reference for line-by-line prose in Little Kettle Valley: Josie's letter, first-join chat, finale speeches, NPC dialogue, Patchouli entries, quest text, item lore, bounty text, cellar-wall/book text. Every principle below gets a WHY, a before/after in the pack's own register, and a named source.

---

## 1. Micro-tension — put tension in every line, not just every scene

Donald Maass's core claim: readers stay hooked by "the moment-by-moment, line-by-line, sometimes simmering-beneath-the-surface tension that keeps the reader in a constant state of apprehension" — not about the plot, but about what happens in the next few seconds. It comes from **conflicting or contrasting emotions**, often what a scene "doesn't get said, and what doesn't get done" (Writer Unboxed, "Deconstructing Micro-Tension").

**WHY it works:** discomfort seeks relief, and the only relief on a page is the next line. A character who is simply *sad*, or simply *warm and welcoming*, gives the reader nothing to resolve. A character who is warm and welcoming *while flinching from something* gives the reader a question they can only answer by continuing.

**Before:** *"Halden was sad about the cellar. He missed Josie and didn't want to open the door."*
**After:** *"Halden set the plate on the table like it might still be hot. 'I've been able to read this for four years,' he said, and did not pick it back up."*

The after-line does what Maass describes: it withholds the feeling word entirely and lets contradiction (four years of ability, zero years of action) carry it.

**Source:** Writer Unboxed, "Deconstructing Micro-Tension" — https://writerunboxed.com/2014/10/20/deconstructing-micro-tension/ ; Mythic Scribes interview with Donald Maass — https://mythicscribes.com/interviews/donald-maass/

## 2. Chekhov's gun and specificity — concrete nouns earn their place, generic ones don't

The rule usually gets flattened to "don't introduce a gun you won't fire," but the sharper craft point is about *noticing*: readers register specific, load-bearing details ("a giant diamond ring, or mysterious briefcase") differently than filler ones ("a fedora"). A writer's job is to be selective about which concrete nouns get the reader's attention, because attention is a promise (Reedsy, "What is Chekhov's Gun?").

**WHY it works:** specificity is what makes prose feel *true* instead of generic — a "cold hearth" is furniture, but "a copper kettle still hanging over a cold hearth" is a fact a reader can picture and later recall when the kettle matters again. Vague nouns (*things, stuff, a mess, some tools*) give the reader nothing to hold and nothing to pay off.

**Before:** *"The house was falling apart, with old furniture and broken things everywhere."*
**After (matches the pack's own canon):** *"a chimney, three walls, a bed frame, and a copper kettle still hanging over a cold hearth."* (`story-final.md`)

Four concrete nouns in a list do more work than one abstract adjective ("run-down") — and the kettle, named once here, is allowed to become the pack's whole visual identity later.

**Source:** Reedsy, "What is Chekhov's Gun? Definitions, Tips and Examples" — https://reedsy.com/blog/chekhovs-gun/ ; MasterClass, "Writing 101: What Is Chekhov's Gun?" — https://www.masterclass.com/articles/writing-101-what-is-chekhovs-gun-learn-how-to-use-chekhovs-gun-in-your-writing

## 3. Vonnegut's rules — every sentence reveals character or advances the action, and start close to the end

From Vonnegut's 8 rules (collected in *Bagombo Snuff Box*): *"Every sentence must do one of two things — reveal character or advance the action."* And: *"Start as close to the end as possible."* And: *"Every character should want something, even if it is only a glass of water."*

**WHY it works:** a sentence that does neither job is dead weight the reader has to read anyway. In a quest book or a book-page journal, where space is capped at ~230 characters, this rule is almost a hard constraint — there is no room for a sentence that's just scenery.

**Before:** *"The valley used to be busier. There were more people, and things were nicer, and everyone got along."*
**After:** *"People leave in February and they do not come back in April."* (`story-final.md`, Josie's cellar note) — one sentence, states a want (people to stay), and it's the whole engine of the plot.

**Source:** BookBaby Blog, "Kurt Vonnegut's 8 Rules For Writing A Short Story" — https://blog.bookbaby.com/how-to-write/kurt-vonneguts-8-rules-for-writing-a-short-story ; Goodreads collection of the 8 rules — https://www.goodreads.com/author_blog_posts/16330105-vonnegut-s-8-rules-for-the-short-story

## 4. King on adverbs — the verb and the noun should already carry the feeling

Stephen King, *On Writing*: *"I believe the road to hell is paved with adverbs... they're like dandelions. If you have one on your lawn it looks pretty and unique. If you fail to root it out, you find five the next day."* He is strictest about adverbs on dialogue tags — "he admonished gravely" — insisting the verb should already do the work.

**WHY it works:** an adverb next to a weak verb is the writer explaining the effect instead of causing it. "She said sadly" tells the reader how to feel; a better verb or a better action shows them and lets them feel it themselves.

**Before:** *"'I've missed you,' Josie said warmly and lovingly."*
**After:** *"'I've missed you,'* Josie said, and put the kettle on before either of them could say anything else.

The action (kettle, immediately, wordlessly) replaces both adverbs and does more.

**Source:** The Marginalian, "Stephen King on Writing, Fear, and the Atrocity of Adverbs" — https://www.themarginalian.org/2013/03/13/stephen-king-on-adverbs/ ; Goodreads quote page — https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/430289-i-believe-the-road-to-hell-is-paved-with-adverbs

## 5. Elmore Leonard's ten rules — get out of the way of the story

Leonard's rules (NYT, 2001), most relevant ones for this project: *"Never use a verb other than 'said' to carry dialogue"* · *"Never use an adverb to modify the verb 'said'"* · *"Keep your exclamation points under control"* · *"Try to leave out the part that readers tend to skip."* His summary rule: *"If it sounds like writing, I rewrite it."*

**WHY it works:** decoration (fancy dialogue tags, exclamation points, throat-clearing description) is the writer's presence showing through the prose. The goal for NPC dialogue and quest flavor text is invisibility — the player should feel like they're hearing Halden, not reading Josh's prose style.

**Before:** *"'You found it!' Halden exclaimed excitedly, gesturing wildly at the plate."*
**After:** *"'You found it,' Halden said."*

Cut the exclamation, cut the adverb, cut "gesturing wildly" — the plate itself, and what Halden does next, carries the excitement.

**Source:** Farnam Street, "Elmore Leonard: 10 Rules Of Writing" — https://fs.blog/elmore-leonard-10-rules-of-writing/ ; Goodreads, "Elmore Leonard's 10 Rules of Writing" — https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/859230.Elmore_Leonard_s_10_Rules_of_Writing

## 6. Le Guin on rhythm — vary sentence length, don't thump

Ursula K. Le Guin, *Steering the Craft*: *"the test of a sentence is, Does it sound right?"* She explicitly "revolts against the Strunk and White idea that good sentences are always short sentences": *"prose consisting entirely of short, syntactically simple sentences is monotonous, choppy, irritating... the thump-thump beat gives it a false simplicity that soon just sounds stupid."*

**WHY it works:** even, chopped-up sentences read as flat because the reader's ear (silently) hears no variation — nothing to signal emphasis. One short sentence after several longer ones lands *because* of the contrast; the same short sentence in a row of short sentences lands on nothing.

**Before:** *"The lamps were dark. Josie counted them. She was young. She promised herself she'd light them one day."*
**After:** *"The Lantern Road — forty lamp posts from the mill to the square to the lake. Josie counted them on her fingers as a child. Every one of them is dark."* (`story-final.md`, adapted rhythm) — two longer, information-carrying sentences, then one short flat statement that lands like a door closing.

**Source:** Literary Hub, "A Writing Lesson from Ursula K. Le Guin" — https://lithub.com/a-writing-lesson-from-ursula-k-leguin/ ; Allison Wall, "Steering the Craft, Part 1: SOUND" — https://allison-wall.com/2019/06/29/2632/

## 7. Subtext in dialogue — say the opposite, or answer a different question

Craft consensus (Helping Writers Become Authors, Medium/Diane Callahan, and others): subtext is "the gap between what a character says and what they mean." The concrete techniques: have the character say the *opposite* of what they feel, or answer a *different question* than the one asked, and let action or silence contradict the words — "I'm fine," while slamming a door.

**WHY it works:** dialogue that states the feeling directly (*"I'm worried the valley will die without me"*) reads as a synopsis of the character, not the character. Dialogue that dodges, deflects, or answers sideways forces the reader/player to do the work of inference — which is what makes a line feel like it came from an actual person with something to protect.

**Before:** *"Halden said, 'I'm scared to open the cellar because it means Josie is really gone.'"*
**After:** *"Josh asks Halden if he's ready. Halden picks up the plate, sets it back down, and says, 'Ask me in the spring.'"*

He never names the fear; he answers a different question (timing, not readiness) and the dodge itself is the tell.

**Source:** Helping Writers Become Authors, "How to Write Subtext in Dialogue" — https://www.helpingwritersbecomeauthors.com/subtext-in-dialogue/ ; Diane Callahan (Medium/The Startup), "Writing Subtext in Dialogue" — https://medium.com/swlh/writing-subtext-in-dialogue-448b1d3884f2

## 8. And-then vs. But/Therefore — causation, not sequence

Trey Parker and Matt Stone's rule from a 2011 NYU talk: boring beats are connected by "and then"; every beat of a working story is connected by **"therefore"** (this causes the next thing) or **"but"** (this complicates or reverses the next thing). Their example of the failure mode: *"Jimmy went to the mall, and then he realized he was tired... and then he got a coffee, and then he walked back... and then he drove home."*

**WHY it works:** "and then" is a list; "but/therefore" is an argument. A quest chain, a letter's paragraphs, even a single sentence with two clauses reads as inert when the second half is just *next*, and reads as propulsive when the second half is caused by or complicates the first.

**Before (and-then structure):** *"Josie built the power plant, and then it ran well, and then winter came, and then she shut it down."*
**After (therefore/but structure):** *"The Works ran for eleven days — **and it worked**, warm rooms and a lit road — **but** Josie shut it down herself, on purpose, before anyone had to ask why."*

**Source:** David Perell, "The But & Therefore Rule" — https://perell.com/note/but-therefore-rule/ ; Go Into The Story (Scott Myers), "Writing Advice from Matt Stone and Trey Parker" — https://gointothestory.blcklst.com/writing-advice-from-matt-stone-and-trey-parker-30941b2cd98c

## 9. End on an image or a question, not a summary

Craft consensus, echoed across the mystery-vs-confusion sources below and the micro-tension sources above: a paragraph or scene that ends by restating its own point (*"...and that's how Josie kept the valley together"*) drains the tension it just built. Ending on a concrete image, an unanswered question, or an action mid-motion keeps the reader's forward pull intact into the next line.

**WHY it works:** summary is closure, and closure releases the tension micro-tension exists to build (see §1). An image or a question is an open loop — the exact thing that makes a reader (or player) tap "next page."

**Before:** *"Josie loved the valley and worked hard to keep everyone together, which is why they all cared about her so much."*
**After:** *"...and I stood in the lane at ten o'clock at night in February and every lamp on the road was lit and I have never been happier in my life."* (`story-final.md`) — ends on the image (lit lamps, a woman standing alone in the cold), not a stated moral.

**Source:** same craft consensus as §1 and §7 above (Writer Unboxed, Helping Writers Become Authors) applied to structure rather than sentence.

## 10. Mystery vs. confusion — withhold information on purpose, never by accident

Shannon Hale and Craft Literary both make the same distinction: **mystery** is when the writer knows exactly what's happening and reveals it "one piece of the puzzle at a time"; **confusion** is when the reader doesn't know where they are, whose side they're on, or what's real, because the writer hasn't decided either. "Being confused in a book makes us angry — facing a mystery makes us intrigued" (Craft Literary, "Mystery vs. Confusion").

**WHY it works:** withheld information is only tension-building if the reader can feel that an answer exists and is coming. If the reader can't even tell what the *question* is, withholding reads as sloppiness, not craft — especially dangerous in a game built for a player (Josh's wife) who "needs a clear path" and dislikes not knowing what to do next.

**Before (confusion — unclear what's even being asked):** *"There's something about the cellar. Halden knows things. Maybe you should talk to him sometime."*
**After (mystery — the question is explicit, the answer is what's withheld):** *"Halden has been able to read Josie's shorthand for four years. He has never once opened that door."* (`story-final.md`, Q54) — the player knows exactly what's unknown (what's behind the door) and exactly who has the answer; only the content of the reveal is withheld.

**Source:** Craft Literary, "Mystery vs. Confusion" — https://www.craftliterary.com/2018/08/27/mystery-vs-confusion/ ; Shannon Hale, "Confusion vs. mystery" — https://shannonhale.com/blog-archive/2009/02/20/confusion-vs-mystery-2

## Banned tells of flat writing

Cross-referenced against every source above — these are the fingerprints of prose that isn't doing the job:

1. **Explaining the feeling instead of causing it** — "she was sad," "he felt nervous" (violates §1, §4, §9). Show the contradiction or the action; let the reader supply the word.
2. **Generic adjectives standing in for specific nouns** — "a nice house," "some old stuff," "a scary door" (violates §2). Name the actual thing.
3. **Summary where a scene belongs** — "they talked for a while about the old days" instead of one real exchange (violates §9, Vonnegut's rule 4).
4. **Even, undifferentiated rhythm** — every sentence roughly the same length and shape (violates §6). Read it aloud; if it thumps, break it.
5. **Everything resolved, nothing left open** — a line, scene, or letter that answers every question it raises (violates §1, §9, §10). Leave one door shut on purpose.
6. **"And then" chains** — events that are merely sequential, not causal or complicating (violates §8). Every beat should answer "so what happened because of that" or "but what went wrong."
7. **Adverbs doing the verb's job** — "said sadly," "walked slowly and carefully" (violates §4, §5). Fix the verb, cut the adverb.
8. **Dialogue that states the subtext out loud** — a character announcing their own motive or fear (violates §7). Make them dodge, deflect, or answer sideways instead.
9. **Confusion mistaken for mystery** — the reader can't tell what question is even being asked (violates §10). The unknown must be a *named* unknown.
10. **Decorative dialogue tags and punctuation** — "he exclaimed!", "she interjected wryly" (violates §5). "Said" is invisible; let it stay invisible.

## The 10 rules to hand a writer on this pack

1. **Put a small contradiction in every line that matters** — a feeling and an action that don't match. That gap is the tension (Maass).
2. **Name the object, not the category.** "Copper kettle over a cold hearth," never "some old furniture" (Chekhov's gun / specificity).
3. **Every sentence reveals character or moves the story. If it does neither, cut it** (Vonnegut).
4. **Kill the adverb; fix the verb underneath it** (King, Leonard).
5. **Let "said" stay invisible. No exclaiming, no interjecting wryly** (Leonard).
6. **Vary the sentence length on purpose — short sentences only land after long ones** (Le Guin).
7. **Make characters answer sideways.** A dodge or a different question beats a stated feeling (subtext).
8. **Connect beats with "but" and "therefore," never "and then."** Every event should complicate or cause the next (Parker/Stone).
9. **End on an image or a question — never on a stated moral** (composite: Maass + mystery/confusion craft).
10. **If the reader can't name what's being withheld, that's confusion, not mystery — fix it before shipping the line** (Hale, Craft Literary).
